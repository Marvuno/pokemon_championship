Pokemon Champion - Graphical Interface Guide

This is a desktop front end for the game, built with PySide6 (Qt). It
runs the existing game logic in Scripts/ without changing any of it:
same damage rolls, same AI, same tournament structure. What changes is
that you click instead of typing numbers, and battle state that used to
be scrolling text is now HP bars, chips, and a live weather-reactive
arena.

*********************************************************************************

Starting the game

Double-click PLAY.bat. It checks for Python, installs the libraries the
game needs the first time (including PySide6, the GUI toolkit), then
asks whether you want the graphical interface or the classic command
line. Pick 1 for graphical, 2 for command line.

From a terminal, the same two options are:
    python play.py       graphical interface
    python main.py       classic command line

*********************************************************************************

What the interface adds

Move cards
Each move is a card showing its type, category, power, accuracy, and the
effectiveness the game itself calculated - SUPER, RESISTED, IMMUNE - with
a type-coloured spine. Hover a card for recoil/multi-hit detail that
doesn't fit on the card face. The bracketed number is its keyboard
shortcut, though clicking is the primary way to play throughout.

Live battle state
HP bars animate down rather than jumping, with type and status chips,
stat-stage changes, protection, charging moves and disabled moves shown
on each Pokemon's card. A Pokemon at 0 HP is marked FAINTED with a red
outline, not left showing a stale number.

Weather-reactive arena
The battleground's background changes with the field's weather - rain
streaks, drifting sand haze, hail, a warm sunny glow - cross-fading
between looks rather than cutting.

Battle feed and log
The panel on the right has two tabs. Battle (the default) shows a
readable, colour-coded feed of what just happened - moves, abilities,
status effects, turn dividers, weather changes - built from the same
data the toast popups over the arena use. Log is the game's raw
narration text, for when you want to see exactly what it said; a small
count badge appears on the tab if something lands there while you're
looking at Battle.

Toasts
The same events in the Battle feed also flash briefly over the arena as
they happen, so you don't have to glance at the side panel mid-fight to
notice something important.

Team trimming and keeping Pokemon
When a round asks you to pick exactly how many Pokemon to leave behind,
or lets you choose which to keep between rounds, that's a proper
multi-select screen - tap to toggle, a running count, one Confirm - not
the game's real one-at-a-time prompt loop repeated on screen.

Your Team, Scout Opponent, Match History
"Your Team" (top right) opens a separate window with stats, ability and
full moveset for every Pokemon on your team, kept live during battle.
The opponent's side of that window stays hidden until you have earned
it -- by winning the round, or by a successful scout in "Scout
Opponent". Choosing "Scout Opponent" or "Check History" from the
in-game menu opens a window rather than printing paragraphs into the
log: Scout Opponent as the competitor's artwork with the scouting
report beside it, Check History as your record against them plus the
scoreline of every previous meeting. Neither goes to the log at all --
the log is a transcript, not a screen you should have to read.

Career History (the title screen's HISTORY option)
One window, five tabs, all of it there when it opens:

  Opponents      every competitor, clickable, with tier and rating
  Champions      who won each championship
  Career         the one you picked: portrait, titles, W/L, win rate
  Tournaments    every championship they entered and where they came
  Head to Head   their record against everybody, hardest opponent first

Click a name and the last three tabs fill in; click another and they
change. Everything scrolls vertically -- nothing is ever laid out wide
enough to need a sideways drag. You are never asked to confirm anything:
the interface answers the engine's own questions, and closing the window
returns you to the title screen.

Head to Head is ordered by the opponent's rating rather than by how
often they were played, so it reads as "how far up the field have they
actually beaten anyone" instead of reflecting who the bracket happened
to keep drawing.

A competitor who has never played says so on all three of those tabs,
rather than leaving the last one's record on screen.

The portraits are full illustrations with the name, write-up, quote and
ace Pokemon set into them, so the career tab gives the picture the
larger half of the window rather than repeating the write-up as text
beside it.

Pokedex (top bar, any time)
One search box over three sections: Pokemon, Moves and Opponents. It is
reference material only -- it reads a snapshot and touches no game state
-- so it is available from the title screen, the pre-battle menu and
mid-battle alike.

The search takes one line of text rather than a screen of dropdowns.
Bare words match a name, a type or an ability; anything else is
`key:value` or `stat>number`, and a leading `-` excludes:

    fire flying              both types
    tier:ultra               by tier
    spd>130                  by stat (spd is Speed, spdef the other one)
    move:earthquake          learns it
    ability:levitate
    total>550 custom:yes
    water -tier:boss
    cat:special pwr>110      on the Moves tab
    rating>300               on the Opponents tab

An unknown key matches nothing rather than everything, so a typo shows
an empty list instead of the whole roster.

In the list on the left a Pokemon's typing is shown as small colour
blocks rather than words -- "STEEL DRAGON" as two chips was most of the
row and left the longer names nowhere to go, while two swatches say the
same thing in a fifth of the width and three stack just as well. Hovering
one names them. Long names elide rather than pushing the blocks out of
sight.

A Pokemon shows its sprite, typing, tier, abilities, base stats as bars
filled to where each one sits across the whole roster, and its full move
pool. Competitor portraits are sized from the display, so they are as
large as the window can hold on a 1440p monitor and still fit a 1366x768
laptop -- 576px on a 1280x800 screen, which is 864 real pixels of a
1024px source. The search box carries its examples as a tooltip and the
Close button shares the top row, because that hundred pixels of chrome is
a hundred pixels the artwork can have instead. Clicking a portrait opens
it full size: just the picture, centred on a dimmed screen with no panel
or title bar around it, and a click anywhere puts it away again. A move
shows type, category, power, accuracy, PP, priority, what it actually
does in plain English, and everyone who learns it -- and is marked CUSTOM
if it is one of this game's own rather than a real one. A competitor
shows their portrait, rating, tier, character ability, quote, their
write-up and their Strategy.

What it deliberately does *not* show: which competitor brings which
Pokemon, in either direction. That is what Scout Opponent's scouting
roll is for, so it is not in the snapshot at all and no query can reach
it.

Compare (the reward screen)
Winning a round opens this by itself -- there is no yes/no to answer
first. One of yours sits beside one of theirs with name chips at the top
of each column to switch which pair you are looking at, and each shows
typing, tier, sprite, ability, every stat as base + IV = stat, the IV
total, and the full moveset with type, category and power. It replaces
the old separate team viewer, which had the same information one side at
a time.

Each stat bar is scaled to the better of the two, so the longer bar is
the better Pokemon at that stat, and the totals are green or red by
which side wins.

One press does the whole thing. Underneath, the engine asks three
questions in a row -- yes/no, then which of yours, then which of theirs --
and as three identically-labelled Proceed buttons that was genuinely
confusing: nothing said which press was the one that committed. Both
sides are selectable on the one screen instead, and the single press
answers all three; the two index questions are filled in from what was
selected and never reach you.

  Fast Comparison   your weakest against their strongest, for when you
                    just want to know whether you are being offered an
                    upgrade
  Swap these two    give up the one selected on the left, take the one on
                    the right ("Take it" when nothing has to be given up)
  No thanks         keep your team as it is -- or, on the take offer, let
                    the organiser hand you a random one instead

Changing your mind works at any point: declining clears the choice, so
any question the engine asks afterwards is answered with its go-back
sentinel rather than a stale selection. A planned slot the engine is not
actually offering is pulled back into range rather than sent, which used
to make it ask the same question a second time.

Shutting the window counts as declining. This screen empties the action
bar and takes the whole question on itself, so a dismissal that said
nothing left the engine blocked inside input() with no control on screen
able to reach it -- the game stopped dead. It needed catching in two
places, not one: the X arrives as a close event, but Escape never does,
because QDialog turns Escape into reject() and hides the window through
done(). Both routes now decline, and whichever runs first clears the flag
so the answer is sent exactly once. Of the screens that empty the action
bar this is the only one that hands the question to a window rather than
putting its own buttons back, so it was the only one that could strand
you; the HISTORY window has had the same guard from the start.

The window is sized once from the display and its two halves are always
the same width. It used to grow a little every time the selection changed
-- six long names in the picker row, or a Pokemon with four long moves,
widened one half and the window with it. The picker wraps to three names
a row, long names on chips elide, and the explanation lines are allowed
to be narrow, so nothing inside can stretch it. It is a resize rather
than a fixed size, so it can still be dragged smaller.

If your team is not yet full the round is a straight pick with nothing
given up, and the window says so; with a full team it says that taking
one of theirs means giving one of yours away.

Proceeding closes the window and goes straight on to the next matchup.
The "press any key to continue" the engine prints after a reward is
answered for you -- it is a keypress between finishing a swap and seeing
the next opponent that exists only because the terminal version needed
it. Ordinary continue prompts still wait for you.

When the other side switches
A switch used to be silent: their sprite simply became a different
Pokemon between one turn and the next, and you found out by looking. Now
the moment the Pokemon on the field changes, that side's status card
flashes its own colour for a second and a line goes into the turn feed
naming who came in -- "Expert Cynthia sent out Milotic." Your own
switches are announced the same way. The first Pokemon of a match is not
a switch and is not announced, and a new match never announces its lead.
Three switches inside one turn all get their own line.

What is no longer printed
The engine was written for a terminal, and printed its whole working
state after every turn -- status, stat stages, volatile conditions,
entry hazards, charging counters, base stats, a drawn HP bar, the
occasional bare number, and the caret it used to show it wanted you to
type. All of that is on screen as an actual widget now, so it is
filtered out of the log on the way through the bridge. With the drawn
artwork going the same way (below), that is now about half of everything
the engine prints during a run -- 2,027 lines of 4,184 in a five-battle
playthrough. The share moves about between runs, because it depends on
how many turns a run takes against how many menus it opens. The
narration -- who used what, what was effective, what fainted -- is
untouched. The terminal build still prints the lot; nothing was removed
from the engine, only from the window.

The drawn artwork goes too. The engine prints a block-letter title and
rules a frame around every bracket entry -- three lines per entry, two of
them pure box characters, 640 lines of frame in a five-battle run. The
log is a transcript to be read back and searched, not a screen, so the
frames and the block letters are dropped and the row inside each frame is
rewritten as its columns:

    ╔====╦══════════════════╦===╦===╗
    ║ 1  ║ Expert Cynthia   ║ A ║ 346 ║      becomes    1  Expert Cynthia  A  346
    ╚====╩══════════════════╩===╩===╝

Nothing that carries information is lost -- only the ruling around it. The
test for a droppable line is that it contains no letters and no digits at
all, so a line with any real text on it can never match, and the check
runs on the colour-stripped line so a coloured frame is still a frame.

This is the log alone. capture() takes its copy at the top of _write,
before any of the filtering, and the title screen and the tutorial read
through capture() -- so they still show the artwork as drawn. Only the
transcript is plain.

The caret needed catching twice. Most output reaches the log through
_write, which scrubs it, but a question is put there by hand in
Bridge._ask so it can be shown alongside its own answer buttons -- and
that path skipped the scrub, so "--> " came through on the end of every
prompt, 115 times in five battles. _ask scrubs its own copy now. The
InputRequest still carries the prompt exactly as the engine wrote it,
because the parser reads the option list out of it.

One line *was* removed outright: after a Pokemon moved, the game
reported the status of the Pokemon it had attacked -- "Garchomp is still
Normal." It named the wrong side to be useful and said nothing at all in
the usual case where the status was Normal.

Hovering a Pokemon in battle
Hover either sprite, or one of the little balls on a status card, and a
small panel appears with that Pokemon's four moves (colour-coded by
type, with category and power) and its six IVs, each IV shown over the
stat it produced. A 31 is picked out in cyan.

Your own side always shows. Theirs shows only once you have earned it,
the same two ways as the team window -- until then the panel names what
is on the field and tells you to try Scout Opponent. The balls are the
only way to reach their *bench* mid-battle, which is most of what a
successful scout buys you.

Settings
The "Settings" button (top right) has difficulty -- Normal, or Beginner
where every opponent plays the simple attacking AI -- and it takes
effect the next time you start the game. Music volume is on the same
panel.

The window is borderless fullscreen; there is no windowed mode and no
size option, so the arena and the sprites are laid out once against the
screen you actually have.

Credits
On the top bar, not thrown up automatically. They used to open themselves
the moment a run ended, over the top of the final standings -- a window
to dismiss before you could look at anything else. Whether you won, and
how many titles you hold, is remembered from that moment so the closing
note still reads correctly whenever you come to it.

Fallback text entry
Every screen has a small "or type a value" field in the action bar. It's
deliberately understated - buttons are the primary way to play - but it
stays available as an escape hatch if a button ever doesn't cover what
you're trying to do.

*********************************************************************************

Requirements

Python 3.8 or newer.
PySide6 (Qt for Python) - required for the graphical interface.
pygame, numpy, openpyxl - already required by the game.

PLAY.bat installs all of these for you. Manually, that's:
    pip install -r requirements.txt

How it is wired up (for anyone touching the code)

GUI/                toolkit-agnostic pieces, usable by any front end:
  bridge.py           runs main() on a worker thread, redirects its I/O,
                      and hooks a handful of game functions to know what
                      is being asked and what just happened
  prompt_parser.py    turns terminal prompts into structured choices
  ansi.py             ANSI escape parsing for the raw log
  theme.py            palette, type colours, status maps, size scale
  codex.py            the Pokedex's data and its query language -- no Qt,
                      so the search is testable on its own. sprite_key
                      lives here: it is the one implementation, and
                      bridge.py imports it rather than having a second

GUI_qt/             the Qt rendering layer
  main_window.py      the window: scoreboard, arena, tabbed feed/log,
                      action bar, every prompt renderer
  widgets.py          RoundedPanel base, HPBar, Chip, MoveCard,
                      CombatantCard, Banner, FieldBoard, ScoutCard,
                      ActionButton
  panels.py           RosterDialog, OpponentInfoDialog, HistoryDialog,
                      CareerDialog, CompareDialog, StandingsDialog,
                      StoryDialog, SettingsDialog, CreditsDialog
  pokedex.py          the Pokedex window (three sections, one search box)
  arena.py            the weather-reactive battleground backdrop
  sprites.py          sprite loading via QMovie
  fonts.py            resolves theme.py's font stacks into QFonts
  settings.py         persistent settings via QSettings (difficulty)

Three redirections make a terminal program clickable: print() appends to
the log/feed, input() blocks the game's worker thread until a button is
clicked, and os.system('cls') clears the log. On top of that, bridge.py
wraps a number of game functions - battle_setup, select_move,
switching_criteria, check_win_or_lose, move_order_and_execution,
UseAbility, team_selection, save_game, about_opponent, check_history,
round_begin, scoreboard, elo_rating and others - so the interface knows
what screen to show and when. Every wrapper calls the original function
unchanged; none of the wrapped functions had their own behaviour altered.

Two small behaviour changes exist, both to keep the game running rather
than to change how it plays: a missing audio file or device can no
longer end a run, and restart() signals the interface to relaunch
cleanly instead of calling os.execl over the running process.

Artwork is scaled to *device* pixels and stamped with the ratio
(_FittedArt in panels.py), and the animated sprites decode at device
resolution too (show_sprite in sprites.py -- QMovie has no
setDevicePixelRatio, so the frames are scaled up and the label fits them
back into its logical rect).

Two rules follow from that, and both were learned the hard way. First,
show_sprite gives its label the sprite's own size, because scaled
contents in a label bigger than the sprite stretches it -- a 190px box
was showing a 370px-wide distorted Pokemon. Callers wanting a stable
box put the label inside a fixed-size container and centre it. Second,
_FittedArt carries a QSizePolicy of Ignored so the layout sizes the
picture rather than the reverse, which also means a layout will not
reserve room for it: it needs the same container treatment, or the
widget beside it is laid out straight over the top. Qt lays out in logical pixels, so on a
150%-scaled display a portrait fitted to a 680px label was being
stretched to 1020 real pixels by the compositor -- a third of the detail
thrown away before the screen saw it, from 1024px source art that had
it to spare. That, not the source resolution, was why the portraits
looked soft.

If the game itself raises an exception, the interface shows it in a
dialog with a copy button instead of closing silently.

The save
savefile.json, written by Scripts/Game/savefile.py. It holds the facts
that need to outlive a run -- your rating, participation, titles, the
Pokemon you kept and what was rolled for them, and everyone's records
-- and rebuilds the rest from Data/pokemon.csv and Data/competitors.csv
on load, so it is safe to open and edit by hand. An older savefile.dat
(the previous pickle format) still loads and is migrated on the way in;
it is left alone afterwards, so it stays as a backup.
